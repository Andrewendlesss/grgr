# Alice & Daniel — creative recommendations
Date: 22 September 2026  
Status: First-pass direction proposal. This is the requested recommendations stage, not a finished story bible or production specification.

## Recommendation

Develop **What the Water Keeps** (working title), a premium, mobile-first cinematic narrative puzzle adventure. Target a first playthrough of roughly three to four hours. Use illustrated environments with shallow 3D depth, deliberately framed cameras, a small recurring cast, and touch interactions built around reflections.

The creative promise: **A man builds a machine to reach the woman he lost. To find her, the player must reconstruct a life that was larger than his memory of it.**

The emotional question: **Can Daniel continue loving Alice without continuing to keep her?**

The commercial hook should be visible in motion: slide a reflection across an abandoned dock and reveal the same place during the summer they met. That immediately communicates a playable idea, a visual identity, and a mystery.

All titles, ages beyond those supplied, character additions, scope figures, and plot mechanisms below are proposals. Nothing here establishes a budget, sales forecast, final title clearance, or shipping commitment.

## Three viable directions

| Direction | What the player does | Main strength | Main risk | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cinematic reflection puzzle adventure | Explores small scenes, aligns reflections from different years, reconstructs events, and makes a few consequential choices | Makes the story's relationship with memory physically playable | Puzzles could interrupt emotion unless each reveals something meaningful | Best overall fit |
| Interactive illustrated drama | Performs everyday gestures, participates in conversations, and follows an authored relationship across time | Most room for natural dialogue and domestic intimacy; smallest proposed environment scope | Needs a distinctive interaction beyond advancing text | Best fallback if resources are very limited |
| First-person temporal mystery | Explores a lakeside settlement, examines machines and evidence, and connects three timelines | Strong physical presence and room for spatial revelations | Adds navigation, camera, animation, and performance work to a mobile project | Consider only after a convincing prototype and a larger production plan |

Keep the first direction's visual scale modest and its emotional scale large. A dock can be spectacular when the player has known it for twenty years.

Two useful references are supported by their publishers' descriptions: [Florence](https://annapurnainteractive.com/games/florence) uses relationship vignettes as gameplay, while [Gorogoa](https://annapurnainteractive.com/games/gorogoa) uses arranging and combining illustrated panels as its puzzle language. Their official pages link mobile and desktop releases. These are useful format references, not evidence of likely sales for this project. Our reflection system, cast, plot, artwork, and puzzle grammar should have their own identity.

## The requested cinematic influence

Use interwoven time spans, a concrete physical procedure, information that changes the meaning of an earlier scene, subjective experience under pressure, and an intimate relationship carrying large speculative stakes.

The story should still be understandable on its first playthrough. A reveal should make the player reconsider an event, not require an explanation video to understand who is alive.

Build toward three strands meeting at the water:

- **The summer — age thirteen:** several weeks of friendship and discovery.
- **The afternoon — age twenty-two:** the ordinary day Alice dies, shown in separated pieces.
- **The night — proposed age thirty-four:** Daniel's final attempt to reach her.

Introduce the era plainly on its first appearance, then distinguish it with clothing, geography, sound, and the state of familiar objects. Color helps but must not carry timeline information alone. The ledger offers a short, optional recap for someone returning after a week away.

Keep one main reversal: the crying girl at the dock had seen a fragment of this final encounter. Supporting discoveries deepen that reversal. Avoid adding a secret identity, an evil scientist, a cosmic war, and a second mastermind around it.

## What to preserve from the original

Preserve the first stone disturbing the reflection; Daniel's compulsive ledgers; Alice's defensive humor; her sudden death during an unremarkable afternoon; the machine genuinely reaching her; the silent view of empty places; the offer of “Just one more”; and the three outcomes.

The final departure must leave Alice dead. Morning must arrive without proving that his pain was useful or that he will never relapse into grief.

Keep the small domestic ending after the grand climax. He writes, sleeps, and gets up again. That movement gives the entire story its scale.

## Changes that would make the relationship stronger

### Alice needs a life Daniel cannot summarize

Alice is human. Her first vision does not make her an emissary of the lake or someone born to teach Daniel how to grieve.

Give her a practical ambition: she wants to become a location sound recordist. At thirteen she borrows equipment, records ridiculous noises, and makes bad little radio plays. As an adult she earns a small paid job away from town. This provides objects and sound design without making every hobby a metaphor.

Her flaw is avoidance. She can be funny when she should apologize, delay an uncomfortable conversation, and turn sincere praise into an argument. Sometimes she misjudges someone. Sometimes Daniel understands a situation better than she does.

She should have scenes in which Daniel is absent. Recommend two short playable Alice scenes: one recording session with her friend and one decision about work. Her independent life is established directly, rather than only discovered by searching her possessions after she dies.

Do not impose “two or three truths per conversation” as a formula. In some conversations she should want batteries, be annoyed about money, or simply make a joke. Reserve the accidental honesty for moments with pressure behind them.

### Daniel's keeping must begin as something valuable

His ledger helps people. He knows which neighbor needs a repaired radio, remembers a friend's interview, and saves a receipt Alice actually needs. His attentiveness is part of why she loves him.

The trouble grows when attention becomes control: he revises an account of a fight until it sounds fair to him, keeps a broken object after its owner wants it gone, or assumes that knowing someone's habits means knowing what they will choose.

Give him a credible practical background in repair work, instruments, or acoustic engineering. The machine should grow out of years of observation and incremental failures. Its impossible function belongs to the fictional lake; it does not require pretending that speculative technology is established science.

### Let the relationship have a real disagreement

At twenty-one, Alice gets work outside town. Daniel offers to solve the practical difficulties without asking whether she wants to leave.

Their argument is about how they make decisions together. Neither is simply correct. She has delayed telling him; he has already built a future around her staying.

They try living apart for a period. They reconnect through effort, including apologies that do not become speeches. On the day she dies, they are looking forward to something modest and unfinished.

This makes their relationship chosen, imperfect, and interrupted. It also prevents the preserved summer from being the only version of them that ever worked.

### Keep her death ordinary and outside the puzzle economy

Daniel almost works through that afternoon, but he chooses to be there. His presence does not prevent her death. This is stronger than making the story hinge on a correctable failure to answer a phone.

Proposed scene: they are sorting laundry or making lunch when she stops mid-sentence. Show the interruption and his immediate attempt to get help. Do not turn resuscitation into a performance challenge, a rewind puzzle, or a replayable death animation.

A sudden medical emergency is sufficient for this recommendations stage. Choose and research a specific cause before writing the final scene. Do not invent symptoms now or later imply that an earlier dialogue choice could have saved her.

Her final words should be ordinary. The importance comes from being unfinished, not from accidentally predicting the ending.

## A small cast with independent lives

Names are provisional.

| Character | Their own concern | Relationship to Daniel and Alice | Dramatic function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ruth, Daniel's mother | Keeping a small repair shop solvent and deciding when to retire | Affectionate, practical, occasionally impatient with both of them | Establishes a home that matters beyond the romance |
| Jonah, their childhood friend | Training for work elsewhere, then raising a family | Shares jokes and old embarrassments with them; eventually refuses another canceled promise from Daniel | Makes the cost to the present visible without giving a lecture |
| Mara, Alice's older sister | Managing an ordinary adult life while navigating her difficult bond with Alice | Knows versions of Alice that Daniel never met | Has an equally legitimate relationship to Alice's belongings and memory |

Avoid turning the supporting cast into grief advisers. Jonah asks Daniel to attend something because Jonah wants him there. Mara wants a recorder back because Alice promised it to her. Ruth can worry about an unpaid invoice in a scene whose larger emotional meaning arrives later.

If Ruth has died before the final night, place that loss years after Alice's and convey it briefly. Do not stage another elaborate death sequence. Jonah and Mara remain alive: Daniel has actual people to return to, rather than a world consisting only of the dead.

Establish the final empty places through ordinary interactions: one damaged dock plank, a tree used to measure stone throws, the rise where Ruth waves. The ending repeats their framing. No translucent figures or explanatory voices are needed.

## Proposed story shape

This is an outline for evaluating direction, not the complete scene plan.

1. **A small impossible event.** Adult Daniel adjusts a receiver beside still water. A sound continues after its physical source stops. He records the result and prepares for a night he has rehearsed for years. Show the machine's operation before explaining its purpose.
2. **The first stone.** At thirteen, he finds Alice crying at the dock. He breaks the reflection with a stone. Their awkward first conversation leads to a practical shared task.
3. **A life worth wanting back.** Summer scenes become friendship; selected later scenes show attraction, work, arguments, and repair. One Alice scene establishes something Daniel never witnessed.
4. **The missing afternoon.** The player discovers that Alice is dead by roughly the first third. The continuing question is what this relationship actually was and what the machine can truly recover. Do not spend most of the game concealing the premise.
5. **A working machine, a narrowing life.** Its tests succeed. Time spent inside an encounter is time absent from the present. Daniel begins treating living people as interruptions. Jonah and Mara have understandable limits.
6. **The scene inside the reflection.** The player recognizes visual and sonic details from the first dock scene in the final apparatus. Alice's childhood tears came from seeing the farewell, not from knowing a medical diagnosis or deliberately arranging the romance.
7. **The empty places.** Daniel reaches Alice. Their conversation converges with the childhood vision before any ending choice. She asks him to turn around. The silent sequence plays, the lake offers one more summer, and the player makes an informed decision.

As a pacing target, let at least a third of the relationship scenes have an immediate concern other than loss or the machine. Humor, irritation, attraction, work, and boredom give the sadness something to act upon.

## Lake rules to settle before the full script

Adopt a small set of rules and demonstrate them through play. These are proposed story rules, not scientific claims.

1. **Place holds the trace across time.** At its shore, the lake can reveal impressions from different times, including a future encounter; it does not observe those times in human chronological order. This permits Alice's first glimpse. Access remains tied to this lake and the lives and places connected to its shore, rather than any reflective surface anywhere.
2. **Stillness reveals; disturbance interrupts.** An unstable glimpse can vanish before its sequence becomes a durable memory. An emotion or sensory association may linger. Disturbing water does not erase ordinary memories.
3. **The machine stabilizes an encounter.** Alice can respond as herself and surprise Daniel. The player must not spend hours reaching her only to discover that every line was a recording fabricated by him.
4. **History remains history.** An encounter cannot prevent her death or carry her physical body into the present. The exact metaphysics of a living impression need a private, consistent answer in the story bible.
5. **The present continues.** Time in the encounter costs real time outside it. Early safe tests establish this. Later danger comes from Daniel extending the connection beyond its demonstrated limits.
6. **The final offer is a distinct bargain.** Accepting “one more” binds Daniel's continuing awareness into a fixed summer. Refusing both the bargain and the exit keeps the temporary connection open until his body fails. These must be different states with different consequences.
7. **Closure is permanent.** The final action can end the lake's ability to make further encounters. Establish that possibility through an earlier irreversible small-scale failure, rather than inventing a destruction switch at the climax.

The childhood vision must show a farewell common to all three endings. It cannot prove that Daniel selects one branch while the player later selects another.

Recommend that thirteen-year-old Alice recognizes overwhelming sadness without understanding the older people or the machine. The stone breaks the image; she loses its sequence. Some unease remains, but the ensuing relationship consists of fresh decisions. “She stayed anyway” becomes a pattern of choosing connection despite unease, not a child consenting in advance to her death.

Do not give the lake a villain speech. Its offer can use Daniel's own familiar phrase. Its behavior is legible even if its ultimate origin is not explained.

## What the player actually does

Use three recurring actions: **observe, align, release**.

- Observe a place and its human details.
- Align a reflection or an object with the corresponding trace of another year.
- Release the alignment to return to the present, then use what was learned.

Add a small number of meaningful ledger entries and conversational responses. Earlier choices change emphasis, remembered details, and the texture of relationships. Do not score “healthy grief,” award affection points, or lock acceptance behind perfect dialogue choices.

Make the ledger useful and beautiful throughout. The story's argument is not that recording a life is wrong. In the ending, the same action changes purpose.

**Example puzzle: the repair-shop drawer.** In the present, a cabinet's concealed catch cannot be seen. The shop stands at the water's edge. From the dock, the player aligns the lake's reflection of its open loading doors with those same doors in summer. Ruth is visible inside, preparing to close. Her routine reveals the catch beneath the counter. The alignment occupies the player's view; releasing it allows the player to leave the dock, enter the present-day shop, and open the real drawer.

The reward is a machine component and a small character beat: Ruth always complains about that catch and always leaves repairing it until next week. Her personality arrives during the observation. Nothing moves physically from the past into the present.

A second version later adds a conflict: Daniel's ledger misremembers which drawer Alice used. Alice's action contradicts him. Progress requires correcting the entry. This demonstrates that his record can be incomplete without making the player choose which person deserves to be forgotten.

Interaction requirements:

- One-finger input; equivalent mouse/controller actions if Steam proceeds.
- A toggle alternative to holding an alignment.
- Large readable text, adjustable subtitle size, transcript, and clear speaker identification.
- Visual equivalents for sound clues; headphone use optional.
- Optional hints and puzzle assistance.
- No timed dialogue choices, reflex gates, or loss of ending options because of dexterity.
- Autosaves at stable scene transitions; reliable resume after interruption.
- A clear final-choice confirmation inside the game's fiction.
- All ending choices available on a first playthrough, with later chapter replay.

The player should never accidentally receive the fatal ending because they paused the game, stopped touching the screen, or answered a phone call.

## Preserve the endings, clarify their differences

### Accept the offer: the summer that cannot become tomorrow

Everyone lost returns inside the lake's bounded summer. Recommend their last complete summer before Alice's death, when both leads were twenty-one and Ruth was alive. Each person appears at their age in that summer; Daniel carries his older awareness into his younger self. The cycle closes before the autumn that followed. The external world is not rewritten. The bargain replaces the responsive final encounter with a fixed replay: Alice and the others live the same sequence again; Daniel alone retains awareness across cycles.

Show the first restart warmly. Then let him anticipate a minor accident or a line of dialogue. His attempt to change the moment meets the bargain's limit. The horror is his growing separation from people who remain at the beginning.

Convey at least one ordinary disappointment within the preserved summer. It is his chosen past, not a romance polished into faultlessness.

Explain the bargain before the player accepts: lasting continuation inside this loop replaces a return to life outside it. Do not present it as successful physical resurrection.

### Refuse to leave: the conversation runs out

Daniel declines the loop but continues the temporary encounter past its safe limit. His body remains in real water. A previously established physical injury explains the spreading stain; the image must not imply a new unexplained disease or late supernatural punishment.

Alice sees the real condition reaching the encounter. She enters the shallows, takes his hand, and stays with him until the water stills. Keep the physical harm non-graphic. The tragedy lies in a life ending while help and living relationships still existed outside the encounter.

Interpret “the first and only time” as the first physical contact of this final encounter. Over a nine-year relationship, forbidding all earlier affectionate touch would require a separate justification and would make their intimacy feel artificially withheld.

Seed that the boundary can briefly collapse as the connection fails. Do not invent the ability to touch solely to decorate the death scene. Her staying with him expresses compassion; it does not establish death as the highest proof of love.

Avoid a triumphant reunion afterward. This ending closes a possibility; it does not unlock a superior paradise.

### Let go: the morning remains

Daniel stands in the water, says goodbye to Alice, and also addresses the thirteen-year-old version of himself who still expects another summer. His younger self does not need to appear as a literal ghost.

He destroys the machine and permanently ends the lake's power. **Recommended interpretation of “destroy the lake”: its impossible memory is destroyed, while ordinary water remains.** This is a proposed refinement, not an unnoticed substitution. It allows the final walk through the same landscape and avoids making acceptance depend on an unexplained act of large-scale destruction.

If literal destruction of the physical lake is essential, the full plan must establish its artificial structure, how it can be destroyed, who is affected, and why the resulting action follows from this story. That is a different production and ethical emphasis.

He walks home through the empty places. Let him answer a mundane message from Jonah or make a small arrangement with Mara. Then he writes, sleeps, and wakes. Grief continues, alongside participation in life.

Retain the author's final sentence as a candidate for the last ledger entry:

> “The water remembered me first — but I remembered them longest, and then I went to bed, and then, in the morning, I went on.”

Its plain final movement is the important part. Whether the whole line remains should be decided in the context of the finished scene.

## Dialogue and performance

Most dialogue should perform an immediate social action: tease, conceal embarrassment, ask for help, evade a question, repair damage, or change the subject. Let the theme emerge from those actions.

Childhood speech should be shorter, more literal, and less polished than the adults' speech. Alice's humor can miss. Daniel can be funny. Neither should speak like a narrator describing the game.

**First meeting, provisional:**

Daniel: “I wasn't throwing it at you.”  
Alice: “I noticed.”  
Daniel: “There's a bench further up.”  
Alice: “Is that where I'm supposed to sit?”  
Daniel: “No. It's just dry.”  
Alice looks at the wet boards beneath her.  
Alice: “Bit late for that.”

**An adult disagreement, provisional:**

Daniel: “I thought we'd decided.”  
Alice: “You put it in the calendar.”  
Daniel: “You were there.”  
Alice: “I was looking for my keys.”

**An ordinary exchange, provisional:**

Alice: “Did you get bread?”  
Daniel: “I got batteries.”  
Alice: “For the toast?”  
Daniel sets the bag down.  
Daniel: “I'll go back.”

**At the final encounter, provisional:**

Daniel: “I had things to ask you.”  
Alice: “I know.”  
Daniel: “I wrote them down.”  
Alice: “Of course you did.”

Allow silence after the last exchange. She does not need to turn his ledger into a lesson. Her later request to turn around is a specific act of directing his attention, an exception to her habitual refusal to manage his decisions.

Read every scene aloud. Remove lines that merely explain what the camera already shows. Save lyrical narration for the ledger and the few moments that can bear it.

## Art, sound, and scale

Use painterly surfaces, restrained animation, expressive posture, and a small number of strong camera positions. Favor weather, changing light, and human activity over constant desaturation. Summer needs ordinary ugly details as well as beauty: bins, repairs, noisy insects, a towel that never dries.

Repeat camera framing across ages so the player's familiarity does the work. Reflections can show an absent structure or person while the solid landscape remains unchanged.

Build a sound palette around water, timber, a shop bell, a recorder's transport noise, and a small melodic phrase. Music can expand when the three strands converge. Preserve enough quiet that its arrival has weight.

Propose three major spectacle sequences: the first convincing reflection transition, the completed machine opening the lake's layers, and the final empty-place sequence. Each should use the established locations.

Selective voice acting is a possible first scope; full voice acting remains a budget decision. Do not write an enormous branching script and assume it can later be fully recorded at negligible cost.

## Product and production recommendation

Sell a complete, finite experience through a premium purchase or a separately evaluated demo/unlock model. Recommend no ads, energy systems, daily rewards, or paid ending access. The purchase should fit the work's emotional rhythm.

Build for Android and iOS from the first playable scene. Keep desktop input compatibility in the design and evaluate Steam after testing the prototype. Do not treat successful desktop playback as proof of mobile performance.

Provisional content envelope:

- Three to four hours on a first playthrough.
- Seven chapters, each containing short resumable scenes.
- Approximately six primary locations reused across years.
- Five principal characters, with incidental roles kept small.
- One main narrative path, local conversational variation, three authored endings.
- Roughly ten to fourteen substantial reflection interactions, with simpler everyday gestures between them.
- Two short Alice viewpoint scenes.
- Offline story play as a design goal.

These are planning constraints to test, not estimates derived from a measured production rate. Staffing, budget, schedule, price, engine choice, final word count, and voice coverage need the prototype and the creator's resources.

For a future narrative prototype, [ink and Inky](https://www.inklestudios.com/ink/) are reasonable candidates: the developer describes a writing-and-testing workflow and engine integrations. The present recommendation does not lock the game to a particular engine.

The main production risk is not the number of endings. It is the amount of bespoke art, animation, interaction, and dialogue needed to make the relationship feel lived in. Reusing locations should deepen meaning and reduce workload simultaneously.

## First prototype and decision gate

Build one fifteen-to-twenty-minute slice before producing the whole game:

1. The first dock encounter.
2. An ordinary task and a short, amusing disagreement.
3. A clear transition to the changed adult location.
4. One complete reflection puzzle.
5. A quiet ending beat that changes how the player understands the location.

Use temporary art where possible, but give one shot and one transition enough polish to test the visual promise. Save final voice recording for after dialogue testing.

Suggested first qualitative test: eight to twelve players unfamiliar with the premise, including people who usually play on phones. Ask them, without explaining the intended answers:

- What did you do with the reflection, and what could it not do?
- What does Alice want in this scene?
- What do you like or dislike about Daniel?
- Which time period are you in now?
- What made you want to continue?
- Did the puzzle help you notice the people or make you impatient to return to them?

A useful provisional gate is that at least eight of ten testers can explain the reflection rule and timeline, and at least seven can identify an Alice desire unrelated to Daniel. These are team-set targets, not industry benchmarks or proof of demand. Watch actual play behavior as well as collecting answers.

If emotional investment is weak, revise the ordinary scenes before adding more lore. If the mechanic is opaque, simplify its rules before adding puzzle variety.

## What the detailed planning stage should produce next

Once this direction is chosen, expand it into:

1. A complete chronological account of what actually happened.
2. A separate player-facing chapter and scene order.
3. Character histories, relationships, speech patterns, and independent goals.
4. A precise lake-and-machine rulebook, including touch, time, recurrence, and permanent closure.
5. A clue table listing introduction, apparent meaning, true meaning, and payoff.
6. A scene-by-scene design tying dialogue, player action, puzzle, art, audio, and emotional purpose together.
7. Fully written opening, key relationship scenes, final encounter, and all three endings, followed by the complete script.
8. A branching and save-state specification with accessibility requirements.
9. An asset inventory and production plan grounded in the first slice's measured workload.
10. A release and positioning plan using current platform requirements and researched commercial assumptions.

**Recommended decision:** pursue the cinematic reflection puzzle adventure under the working title *What the Water Keeps*. Preserve the ending's emotional structure. Build the relationship, the rules, and the ordinary world that make the ending earned.
