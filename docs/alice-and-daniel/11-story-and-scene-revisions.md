# Story revision — contrast, conflict, and earned payoff
Version 1.3 · 22 September 2026 · Editorial decisions implemented in the screenplay; performance remains untested

The revised passages live in [chapters 1, 6 and 7](03a-screenplay-opening-and-endings.md) and [chapters 2–5](03b-screenplay-chapters-2-5.md). This document explains the revision and its next tests. It is not an alternate script or a set of scenes to append to the game.

Sections 1–7 record the v1.2 pass. Section 8 records the focused v1.3 continuation; use the current screenplay and game design where an older interaction description has been replaced.

## 1. What needed changing

The existing draft has a strong ending structure, readable supernatural rules, and affection grounded in ordinary behavior. Its chief dramatic risk was sameness: most people made a dry joke, stated a reasonable concern, and accepted a reasonable answer. The writing often protected a moment from excess before allowing it to become exciting. That would make the few major losses carry too much of the game's emotional weight.

Five specific problems shaped this pass:

1. **Success was too often minimized.** The winch is allowed to work, but little celebration follows the fundraiser, and the largest breakthrough was mostly a short stage direction. The revised fundraiser lets Alice enjoy doing good work while the player's mix continues playing. P12 makes each successful configuration visibly contribute to the eventual contact.
2. **Arguments settled too cleanly.** The packing scene already gave both people a legitimate grievance, but several exchanges sounded like the explanation of a conflict after it had been resolved. The revised scene lets delayed disclosure hurt before an apology can tidy it up. His pleasure in her success and anger at her silence coexist.
3. **Supporting characters were in danger of sounding like helpful boundary notices.** Jonah now makes Daniel stop laying out the next task before explaining the practical cost of staying. He can say “We did” about a successful repair and still leave.
4. **The reunion let Daniel set too much of the agenda.** Alice now interrupts his rehearsed opening, asks what happened to their flat, requests time, and asks for something familiar. She must absorb her own death before she can help him think about his life.
5. **A late question contradicted the relationship already shown.** Asking whether Alice had been coming back from the job treated a settled practical question as an unresolved mystery. The revised question asks for something she truly cannot know: whether their proposed life would have worked.

More intensity does not require another death, villain, betrayal, prophecy, or secret relation. Here it comes from better-motivated friction, visible achievement, and a change in who gets to ask the next question.

## 5. Voice checks to use on the next pass

**Daniel** starts with a manageable fact. “How long have you got?” is his attempt to turn a goodbye into a scheduling problem; “I'm also going to be awful company tonight” is an unusually direct admission. Do not give him a polished account of his own psychology. Let him be pleased when something works.

**Alice** wants different things in different scenes: an audience, a job, a difficult conversation to be over, more time to understand a loss. Her voice is not a machine for witty replies. “Good. I was good that day” gives her unembarrassed pride; “I haven't had lunch yet” gives her an immediate scale for an impossible fact. She can fall silent without becoming a mystic.

**Jonah** knows what work costs. “None. That was me saying goodbye” is clear because Daniel has just missed an ordinary signal. “We did” keeps affection inside the argument. Do not make his boundary sound like an ultimatum to stop grieving.

**Mara** notices the specific error and asks for the specific thing. Keep her already-written fundraiser laugh, family recording project, timetable correction, and shelf invitation. A revision that only makes her sharper would flatten her.

**Ruth** retains a task and a sense of proportion. The cake she slides toward Alice celebrates achievement without an inspirational speech. The joke about the mugs belongs to the people in the room; it is not commentary from a wise narrator.

Most memorable lines should do an immediate job in a relationship. Reserve concentrated lyrical writing for the ledger and a few earned transitions. After a pointed line, prefer an action or honest reply over a second competing maxim.

## 6. Production and pacing limits

These revisions preserve five principal characters, six environment kits, fourteen principal views, twenty-eight shared scene slots, twelve numbered interactions, and exactly two Alice viewpoint scenes. No new side quest, combat, open-world traversal, randomized cast, voice commission, or additional ending is proposed.

Fundraiser success uses the existing mix and background activity. The packing argument uses F01, existing boxes, a bag, tape, and held poses. Jonah's callback uses his coat. Contact spectacle uses existing era layers in D03 plus B03 controls; they appear sequentially and then at a brief, bounded crest. Reunion gestures can be tested with the existing pose cards and position changes. The final art/animation workload still needs measurement; prose direction does not establish that it is free.

Do not stretch the revised dialogue to preserve an advertised duration. The existing 151–153-minute allocation remains an untested hypothesis pending a new read-through and interactive timing pass. When a passage drags, first remove a repeated acknowledgment, repeated observation, or forced wait. Do not cut the first unhurried pleasure simply because it carries less plot information than a lore exchange.

## 7. The next evidence to collect

Table-read C3S3; C4S3 into C4S4; C5S1; and C6S3 through C7S3 before committing final performance assets. The useful questions are concrete:

- After the fundraiser, what did Alice accomplish and what did the player change? If the answer is only “a puzzle was solved,” the audible/social payoff is not clear enough.
- After packing, what is each person angry about? If only one person's grievance is understood, inspect the disclosure timing and hall-booking evidence before adding speeches.
- After Jonah leaves, does the reader believe he still likes Daniel? Can they identify what he needs to do next?
- During final calibration, which action made each change happen? If the crescendo feels detached from the player's work, shorten the delay between a solved channel and its response.
- At the reunion, what does Alice want before Daniel asks for reassurance? If nobody can answer, her shock and initiative still read as decoration.
- At recognition, which earlier clue changed meaning? If a reader says a new fact appeared without warning, compare C1S2 and C7S1 shot by shot.
- Before selecting an ending, can a reader explain repetition, bodily death, reset, and archive loss in ordinary words?
- Where did attention wander, and what did the person expect to happen there? Record the scene and reason; a quiet pause and a redundant instruction need different edits.

An editor's read establishes neither emotional impact nor a compelling game. Observe outside players once the opening proof works, retain actual notes, and revise the responsible scene or interaction instead of promising that greater volume or more tragedy will fix it.
